# Let the garden tell nighttime stories

**An after-dark bat mode is a strong fit for Garden Ink.** I recommend a large chart showing this evening’s recorded bat activity by time since sunset, compared with a recent-night median, accompanied by a small seasonal field note. It would answer two interesting questions: “When does this garden become acoustically active?” and “How does this evening compare?” Two alternatives deserve consideration: simpler seven-night comparison bars, or an illustration-led bat field journal. The source software already supports bat-event records and useful history, although the deployed station has not been checked. Crucially, its inspected ultrasonic detector reports **generic bat activity, not identified bat species**. This report proposes directions for selection; no mockup has been created.

## A sunset-relative profile makes each evening interesting

The recommended screen gives the chart most of the **480 × 800 portrait** canvas: strong bars for completed hours since sunset, crossed by one restrained line showing the median for the same periods on up to seven recent comparable nights. A compact headline could describe the first two completed hours against that same span on previous evenings. Future hours remain blank. The white background, generous margins, editorial typography and a small ink-and-wash illustration would retain Garden Ink’s field-journal character. A dark screen inversion is unnecessary; this is a change of subject and layout.

Sunset provides a more useful reference than fixed clock hours. Research across 323 nights at 14 European sites found that time after sunset, night length and weather contributed differently for different species; there is no universal nightly activity shape to assume. The display should reveal this garden’s recorded pattern. ([Perks and Goodenough, 2020](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/8304/))

The most appealing proposed unit is **one-minute intervals containing at least one qualifying bat detection**. An acoustic study in London used activity minutes partly to reduce inflation from a bat circling a detector. This remains an observation measure: a short call makes that minute count, and it does not measure individual bats, visits or continuous calling duration. ([Brook et al., 2025](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/2688-8319.70151)) This would be a new derived metric, with its exact binning defined before implementation. “Minutes with bat detections” is a suitable chart label, supported by a short explanation.

A small field note would reward attention even on quiet evenings. For autumn, “Bats build fat reserves ahead of winter” provides useful context without claiming that the station observed fattening. Winter could give the note more space: bats generally hibernate, but some wake to move or feed during mild weather. ([BCT seasonal guide](https://www.bats.org.uk/about-bats/a-year-in-the-life-of-a-bat), [BCT unusual-time guidance](https://www.bats.org.uk/advice/im-concerned-about-bats/ive-seen-a-bat-flying-at-an-unusual-time))

## Seven-night bars offer the clearest glanceable alternative

The second concept presents seven dated bars under a heading such as **“The garden after sunset”**, with one concise local observation underneath. Its strength is immediate readability: the viewer sees the recent sequence without decoding a profile. Its tradeoff is that it loses the story of when activity occurred within each evening.

There are two honest versions. One shows completed nights, keeping tonight separate and explicitly incomplete. The other compares every evening over the same elapsed period after sunset as the current report. The latter is more engaging during the evening; the former suits a retrospective “last night” display. A partial tonight bar must never appear directly comparable with full-night totals.

Missing or insufficiently monitored nights should appear as gaps. The display should report how many comparable nights exist, rather than always implying seven. Comparable recording effort and detector conditions matter: BCT recommends consistent survey effort, while BTO notes that recording segmentation itself changes recording counts. ([BCT acoustic survey guidance](https://www.bats.org.uk/our-work/science-research/passive-acoustic-surveys/guidelines-for-passive-acoustic-surveys-of-bats-in-woodland), [BTO acoustic comparison guidance](https://www.bto.org/data/tools-products/acoustic-pipeline/support-hub/understanding-and-optimising))

## An illustrated field journal makes quiet nights worthwhile

The third concept leads with one carefully reviewed bat illustration, a short natural-history note and a modest strip of dated local records. This most closely preserves the current artwork-led display. It is also the strongest fallback when usable history is sparse or the season produces few detections.

A common pipistrelle spotlight could explain that these small bats often forage around gardens and usually leave their roost around twenty minutes after sunset. Those are educational facts, not evidence that this garden’s detector heard a common pipistrelle. ([BCT species account](https://www.bats.org.uk/about-bats/what-are-bats/uk-bats/common-pipistrelle)) The spotlight must remain visibly separate from local observation claims. The inspected detector’s frequency hints are explicitly not species identifications; a locally identified species would require separate verified evidence. ([Detector implementation](/home/simon/Documents/OpenObservatory/src/open_observatory/detectors/ultrasonic.py:499))

Avoid turning the first recorded sound into “emergence”, or ordinary detections into claims about hunting success, roosting or migration. The first sound recorded in the garden establishes a timestamp, not where the bat came from. Any later artwork should follow the original robin’s ink-and-wash reference and undergo anatomy review.

## Source support is promising; deployment still needs checking

Read-only source inspection confirms bat-filtered detection queries, historical timestamps, review and withdrawal fields, solar calculations and recording-coverage information. Garden Ink currently requests and reduces bird records only. Existing analytics also retain some rejected or withdrawn events, so their group totals cannot simply become the display’s chart without reconciling eligibility rules. ([Station API](/home/simon/Documents/OpenObservatory/src/open_observatory/api/app.py:1608), [Analytics policy](/home/simon/Documents/OpenObservatory/src/open_observatory/analytics.py:13), [Garden Ink client](/home/simon/Documents/garden-ink/display/gardenink/client.py:100))

Before implementation, verify the deployed detector and ultrasonic capture rate, coordinates, available history, review policy and comparability across configuration changes. Recording coverage does not prove that every ultrasonic window was analysed. The interface must distinguish no qualifying detections, missing recording and incomplete retrieval; an empty graph cannot establish that no bats were present.

Enter bat mode after sunset **at the next permitted hourly refresh**, preserving the persisted hardware cooldown. Return to the bird layout after sunrise on the same basis. Give each bat night its evening date and let it span midnight; use elapsed UTC time for bins so DST remains correct. Bird “today” must independently keep its existing local-midnight boundary. Show dated historical report cutoffs, never a free-standing clock.

The next decision is which experience to target visually. **Choose the sunset-relative profile with a small seasonal note** for the most informative new mode; choose seven-night bars for maximum simplicity, or the field journal if the artwork should remain dominant. After selection, a labelled sample-data mockup at the actual display size can settle hierarchy and readability before any implementation or deployment work.
